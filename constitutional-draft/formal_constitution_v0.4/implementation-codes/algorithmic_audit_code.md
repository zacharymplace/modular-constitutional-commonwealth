## Purpose

This implementation code defines operational standards for auditing public AI, algorithms, automated decision systems, scoring systems, surveillance technology, public data systems, vendor-operated technology, and other systems that materially influence public authority.

Core rule:

> A system that materially shapes public authority must remain visible, auditable, challengeable, reversible, and subject to meaningful human responsibility.

---

# I. Algorithmic Audit Mandate

## Section 1. Function

Algorithmic audit shall determine whether covered public technology:

1. has lawful authority;
2. operates within its approved purpose;
3. uses appropriate and traceable data;
4. produces sufficiently reliable outcomes;
5. preserves rights and due process;
6. supports meaningful human review;
7. provides usable appeal and challenge paths;
8. remains within its approved risk classification;
9. can be corrected, suspended, or rolled back;
10. complies with public accountability requirements;
11. complies with procurement and vendor-control requirements;
12. avoids hidden or unauditable public authority.

## Section 2. Audit Is Not Authorization

An audit does not itself authorize deployment, continued use, expanded scope, or constitutional legitimacy.

Deployment authority must arise from lawful public authority under the Constitution, controlling schedules, statutes, and other applicable law.

## Section 3. No Algorithmic Sovereignty

No audit process may legitimize a technology system that exercises prohibited sovereign authority or defeats required human responsibility.

---

# II. Covered Systems and Audit Triggers

## Section 1. Covered Systems

This Code applies to systems covered by Schedule G, including:

1. artificial intelligence;
2. algorithms;
3. automated decision systems;
4. risk scoring systems;
5. ranking or prioritization systems;
6. predictive systems;
7. surveillance systems;
8. biometric systems;
9. public data systems;
10. cyber systems where they materially shape public authority;
11. public digital infrastructure;
12. vendor or contractor systems used for public functions;
13. self-learning or self-updating systems.

## Section 2. Mandatory Audit

High-risk and critical systems shall receive algorithmic audit.

## Section 3. Additional Audit Triggers

Audit or re-audit may be required when:

1. a system is first deployed;
2. a risk tier materially increases;
3. model or rule logic materially changes;
4. data sources materially change;
5. deployment context materially changes;
6. affected populations materially change;
7. a vendor materially changes the system;
8. material drift is detected;
9. appeal or reversal patterns indicate possible failure;
10. substantial disparity is detected;
11. a serious rights complaint is filed;
12. a security incident affects system integrity;
13. rollback or emergency suspension occurs;
14. a court, tribunal, integrity body, or technology authority orders review;
15. procurement renewal or major contract modification would extend dependence on the system.

## Section 4. Advisory Label Not Controlling

A system may not avoid audit by being described as advisory, experimental, internal, preliminary, workflow support, vendor-managed, or nonbinding when it materially influences public authority.

---

# III. Audit Independence and Responsibility

## Section 1. Named Audit Owner

Each audit shall identify:

1. responsible audit body;
2. lead accountable auditor or office;
3. responsible agency owner;
4. named human accountability owner for the audited system;
5. vendor or contractor where applicable;
6. appeal or review forum.

## Section 2. Independence

Auditors shall be sufficiently independent from:

1. system developers;
2. operational owners;
3. vendors;
4. procurement evaluators;
5. officials whose performance is materially judged by the audited system.

## Section 3. Conflict Disclosure

Material audit conflicts shall be disclosed and mitigated through recusal, independent review, reassignment, or another lawful safeguard.

## Section 4. No Decorative Audit

An audit is invalid where the auditor lacks sufficient authority, access, competence, time, independence, or evidence to test the material claims under review.

---

# IV. Minimum Audit Access

## Section 1. Required Access

Auditors shall receive sufficient access to evaluate:

1. legal authority;
2. system documentation;
3. decision role;
4. model or rule behavior;
5. data provenance;
6. input and output records;
7. performance testing;
8. error records;
9. bias and disparity testing;
10. appeal and reversal records;
11. human review records;
12. security testing;
13. update history;
14. model or rule version history;
15. vendor change records;
16. rollback capability;
17. known limitations;
18. deployment scope.

## Section 2. Vendor Secrecy

Trade secrecy, proprietary design, vendor control, contract terms, or technical complexity shall not defeat sufficient audit, judicial review, rights protection, or meaningful human review.

## Section 3. Protected Access

Where full public disclosure would create lawful privacy, security, or legitimate trade-secret risk:

1. public disclosure may be narrowed;
2. qualified auditors shall still receive sufficient access;
3. courts and oversight bodies shall retain sufficient review access;
4. public surrogate explanations shall be provided where possible.

## Section 4. Audit Obstruction

Material refusal to provide required audit access is an audit defect and may support suspension, procurement action, tribunal review, or other lawful remedy.

---

# V. Audit Scope

## Section 1. Legal Authority

The audit shall determine whether:

1. the system has identifiable legal authority;
2. the approved purpose matches actual use;
3. delegated or vendor authority remains within lawful bounds;
4. prohibited automated authority has not been created.

## Section 2. Data Review

The audit may assess:

1. data provenance;
2. data quality;
3. completeness;
4. representativeness;
5. timeliness;
6. lawful collection;
7. lawful retention;
8. sensitivity;
9. access controls;
10. known bias or distortion;
11. downstream reuse;
12. changed data sources.

## Section 3. Performance Review

The audit may assess:

1. accuracy where measurable;
2. error rates;
3. false positive and false negative rates where relevant;
4. calibration;
5. stability;
6. failure modes;
7. outcome consistency;
8. performance across affected groups;
9. model or rule drift;
10. operational reliability.

## Section 4. Rights Review

The audit shall assess material effects on:

1. equality;
2. due process;
3. privacy;
4. voting access;
5. benefits;
6. legal status;
7. enforcement;
8. surveillance;
9. public service access;
10. appeal;
11. access to remedy.

## Section 5. Human Review Review

The audit shall test whether human review is:

1. staffed;
2. trained;
3. authorized;
4. timely;
5. documented;
6. accessible;
7. auditable;
8. capable of changing or reversing the automated outcome.

## Section 6. Public Accountability Review

The audit shall verify:

1. public registry entry where required;
2. system card where required;
3. named accountable owner;
4. legal basis;
5. audit status;
6. appeal path;
7. human review path;
8. review or expiration date;
9. known limitations;
10. rollback or suspension history.

## Section 7. Procurement and Vendor Review

Where the system is procured or vendor-operated, the audit shall assess:

1. public audit rights;
2. vendor access obligations;
3. data rights;
4. change-notification requirements;
5. subcontractor transparency;
6. lock-in risk;
7. termination rights;
8. rollback support;
9. beneficial ownership or conflicts where material;
10. procurement representations concerning performance, safety, auditability, or rights compliance.

---

# VI. Meaningful Human Review Audit

## Section 1. Review Quality

Auditors shall determine whether human review materially exists in practice rather than only in policy.

## Section 2. Rubber-Stamp Indicators

Indicators of decorative or ineffective review may include:

1. near-zero override rates without independent justification;
2. insufficient review time;
3. inaccessible source evidence;
4. reviewer workload inconsistent with meaningful assessment;
5. inability to reverse outcomes;
6. mandatory deference to automated scores;
7. missing review records;
8. inadequate training;
9. systematic review delay;
10. review pathways that affected persons cannot practically use.

## Section 3. Capacity Findings

Where review capacity is insufficient, the audit may require:

1. increased staffing;
2. reduced automated scope;
3. additional training;
4. altered workflow;
5. temporary suspension;
6. escalation to another review forum.

---

# VII. Algorithmic Review Metrics

## Section 1. Required Metrics

Rights-impacting systems shall maintain sufficient records to evaluate:

1. appeal rate;
2. reversal rate;
3. modification rate;
4. override rate;
5. measurable error rate;
6. disparity indicators;
7. average review time;
8. unresolved review backlog;
9. complaint volume;
10. model or rule drift events;
11. rollback events;
12. human-review staffing sufficiency;
13. material audit findings.

## Section 2. Metric Integrity

Auditors shall test whether metrics are distorted by:

1. suppressed appeals;
2. hidden or inaccessible complaint channels;
3. narrow reversal definitions;
4. selective denominator choice;
5. missing cases;
6. excluded populations;
7. manipulated reporting windows;
8. undocumented changes to metric definitions.

## Section 3. Privacy

Audit metrics shall protect privacy and avoid unnecessary reidentification risk.

---

# VIII. Versioning, Drift, and Change Control

## Section 1. Version Records

Covered systems shall maintain records sufficient to identify:

1. model versions;
2. rule-set versions;
3. data-source changes;
4. material training updates where applicable;
5. deployment context;
6. risk tier;
7. vendor updates;
8. audit status;
9. rollback points.

## Section 2. Drift Monitoring

Audits may evaluate:

1. performance drift;
2. bias drift;
3. data drift;
4. outcome drift;
5. rights-impact drift;
6. security drift;
7. scope drift.

## Section 3. Material Drift

Material drift shall trigger documented review and may require reauthorization, correction, suspension, rollback, or reclassification.

## Section 4. Unauthorized Change

Material system change outside lawful approval gates is an audit defect.

---

# IX. Self-Learning and Self-Updating Systems

## Section 1. Change Gates

Self-learning or self-updating systems shall maintain approval gates for material changes affecting:

1. rights;
2. legal status;
3. benefits;
4. enforcement;
5. surveillance;
6. public-service priority;
7. data sources;
8. deployment context;
9. risk tier;
10. rollback behavior.

## Section 2. No Silent Expansion

A system may not silently expand authority, data use, deployment scope, or decision role through automated adaptation.

## Section 3. Auditability

Self-updating systems shall preserve sufficient records to reconstruct material changes and their consequences.

---

# X. Audit Findings

## Section 1. Finding Classes

Audit findings may be classified as:

1. informational;
2. curable;
3. material;
4. high-risk;
5. critical.

## Section 2. Required Finding Record

A material finding shall identify:

1. system;
2. responsible agency;
3. accountable human owner;
4. legal authority implicated;
5. evidence;
6. affected population or function;
7. rights impact where applicable;
8. severity;
9. required corrective action;
10. responsible correction owner;
11. deadline;
12. verification method;
13. appeal or review path.

## Section 3. Public Summary

Material audit outcomes shall receive a public summary subject to lawful privacy and security limits.

---

# XI. Corrective Action

## Section 1. Available Actions

Audit findings may require:

1. documentation correction;
2. system-card correction;
3. data correction;
4. human-review correction;
5. retraining or workflow change;
6. scope reduction;
7. heightened monitoring;
8. procurement review;
9. vendor remediation;
10. suspension;
11. rollback;
12. reauthorization;
13. tribunal referral;
14. integrity referral;
15. court referral.

## Section 2. Proportionality

Corrective action shall be proportionate to risk, rights impact, reversibility, severity, affected population, and evidence.

## Section 3. Rights Protection

Where continued operation creates serious rights risk, protection of affected persons takes priority over administrative convenience or sunk procurement cost.

---

# XII. Suspension and Rollback

## Section 1. Suspension Conditions

A high-risk or critical system may be suspended where:

1. legal authority is materially defective;
2. audit access is denied;
3. serious rights harm is plausible;
4. meaningful human review is absent;
5. material unauthorized drift occurs;
6. rollback controls fail;
7. security compromise undermines decision integrity;
8. a court or tribunal orders suspension.

## Section 2. Rollback Readiness

High-risk and critical systems shall maintain a documented rollback or safe-degradation path where technically and legally possible.

## Section 3. Continuity

Rollback planning shall account for continuity of lawful public service without using service continuity as justification for continued unlawful or unsafe operation.

---

# XIII. Appeals, Challenges, and Tribunal Review

## Section 1. Affected-Person Challenge

Affected persons shall have a visible route to challenge material algorithmic influence where required by Schedule F and related law.

## Section 2. Technology Review Tribunal

The Technology Review Tribunal may review:

1. model audit disputes;
2. human review failures;
3. data misuse;
4. algorithmic discrimination;
5. black-box public authority;
6. drift;
7. unauthorized self-learning;
8. rollback disputes;
9. registry noncompliance;
10. public AI procurement disputes.

## Section 3. Tribunal Powers

The Tribunal may:

1. require disclosure;
2. order meaningful human review;
3. require audit;
4. require correction;
5. require system-card correction;
6. suspend unsafe systems;
7. require rollback;
8. refer constitutional questions to the Constitutional Court.

## Section 4. Wrong-Door Protection

Good-faith filings submitted to the wrong public forum shall be routed according to the constitutional one-door filing rules.

---

# XIV. Integrity and Anti-Capture Review

## Section 1. Integrity Referral

Algorithmic audit shall coordinate with the Integrity System where evidence suggests:

1. procurement manipulation;
2. vendor capture;
3. hidden conflicts;
4. revolving-door influence;
5. concealed beneficial ownership;
6. false performance claims;
7. audit obstruction;
8. manipulated metrics;
9. technology procurement lock-in;
10. misuse of public authority.

## Section 2. Influence Ledger

Major public technology adoption may require an Influence Ledger under controlling integrity rules.

## Section 3. No Vendor Control of Audit

A vendor may provide technical evidence but may not control the public audit standard, determine the legal sufficiency of its own audit access, or prevent independent review.

---

# XV. Procurement Integration

## Section 1. Pre-Award Requirements

High-risk technology procurement shall establish before award:

1. audit rights;
2. documentation access;
3. data rights;
4. version disclosure requirements;
5. change-notification requirements;
6. system-card support;
7. human-review support;
8. appeal support;
9. rollback support;
10. termination rights;
11. security obligations;
12. public-record obligations.

## Section 2. Contract Modification

Material vendor or system changes may trigger new audit, procurement review, or reauthorization.

## Section 3. Lock-In Risk

Vendor lock-in that materially defeats public audit, rollback, competition, rights protection, or lawful continuity is a procurement and technology-governance risk.

---

# XVI. Audit Records and Public Interface

## Section 1. Audit Record

The public accountability system shall record, subject to lawful limits:

1. system name;
2. agency owner;
3. accountable human owner;
4. risk tier;
5. audit date;
6. auditor;
7. audit scope;
8. finding summary;
9. corrective-action status;
10. appeal path;
11. suspension or rollback status;
12. next review date.

## Section 2. Accessibility

Public audit summaries shall be understandable to ordinary users and shall not rely solely on technical jargon.

## Section 3. Restricted Information

Where detail must lawfully remain restricted, the public record shall provide sufficient surrogate explanation to preserve accountability without disclosing protected information.

---

# XVII. Audit-the-Auditors

## Section 1. Auditor Review

Public algorithmic audit bodies shall themselves be subject to periodic review for:

1. independence;
2. competence;
3. conflict controls;
4. access sufficiency;
5. consistency;
6. rights sensitivity;
7. corrective-action follow-through;
8. capture risk.

## Section 2. Repeated Failure

Repeated failure to identify material defects, enforce access, or verify corrective action shall trigger heightened oversight.

## Section 3. Public Reporting

Audit-the-auditors findings shall be public subject to lawful privacy, security, and investigation limits.

---

# XVIII. Emergency Technology

## Section 1. Emergency Use

Emergency technology remains subject to Schedule J and does not escape algorithmic audit solely because deployment was urgent.

## Section 2. Accelerated Review

Urgent deployment may use accelerated review where lawful, but shall preserve:

1. named human responsibility;
2. legal authority;
3. system logging;
4. rollback capacity;
5. post-deployment audit;
6. expiration or renewal review;
7. rights remedy.

## Section 3. Normalcy Restoration

Emergency technology shall not become permanent by administrative inertia.

---

# XIX. Federal, State, Regional, and Local Administration

## Section 1. Common Floor

All public technology nodes shall comply with national constitutional floors for:

1. rights;
2. public accountability;
3. meaningful human review;
4. audit access;
5. appeal;
6. rollback;
7. technology safety.

## Section 2. Capacity Gate

A lower-level node may not receive full high-risk algorithmic governance duties until sufficient staffing, records, audit, review, appeal, and technical capacity exist.

## Section 3. Support

Higher-level institutions may provide shared audit capacity without eliminating local accountable ownership.

# XXI. Implementation Boundary

## Section 1. Administrative Function

This Code implements existing constitutional technology-governance duties.

It does not create new sovereign powers.

## Section 2. Human Responsibility Preserved

Technology audit may inform public decisions but may not substitute for legally accountable human or institutional judgment.

## Section 3. No Automated Audit Sovereignty

Automated audit tools may assist evidence collection, testing, comparison, anomaly detection, or reporting.

They may not independently:

1. declare constitutional compliance;
2. impose legal sanctions;
3. remove public authority;
4. determine final rights remedies;
5. authorize continued deployment;
6. override courts, tribunals, auditors, or accountable human owners.

---

# XXII. Final Standard

A covered public technology system should be capable of answering:

1. Who authorized it?
2. What public function does it materially influence?
3. Who is personally accountable for its use?
4. What data and logic materially shape its outputs?
5. How is its performance tested?
6. How are errors and disparities detected?
7. Can an affected person challenge the outcome?
8. Can a real human change the result?
9. Can an independent auditor inspect enough evidence?
10. Can the system be suspended or rolled back?
11. What changed since the last approved version?
12. What happens when the audit finds a serious defect?

If those questions cannot be answered reliably, the system is not ready to exercise or materially influence high-risk public authority.
