## Purpose

This schedule defines the court structure, jurisdiction rules, urgent review paths, remedies, technical expert support, and enforcement mechanisms of the Rights and Justice System.

Core rule:

> Courts protect the constitutional floor. They do not govern ordinary policy where lawful democratic choices remain.

---

# I. Court System Overview

## Section 1. Judicial Bodies

The Commonwealth court system may include:

1. Constitutional Court;
2. High Court or Supreme Appellate Court;
3. Administrative Courts;
4. Fast Rights Tribunal;
5. Election and Party Court;
6. Technology Review Tribunal;
7. other courts or tribunals created by constitutional statute.

## Section 2. Specialized Function

Specialized tribunals exist to provide speed, expertise, and accessible remedies.

They do not replace constitutional review where Kernel, rights floor, framework, emergency, election, or federalism issues are implicated.

## Section 3. Public Routing

The public shall not be required to know the internal structure of the judiciary to access review.

Good-faith filings must be routed to the proper forum.

---

# II. Constitutional Court

## Section 1. Jurisdiction

The Constitutional Court has jurisdiction over:

1. Democratic Kernel disputes;
2. constitutional framework disputes;
3. rights floor violations;
4. federal, state, regional, and local authority disputes;
5. emergency power review;
6. amendment classification disputes;
7. refounding disputes;
8. court structure disputes;
9. election-system constitutionality;
10. party-system constitutional disputes;
11. security-force constitutional violations;
12. algorithmic sovereignty threats;
13. stewardship overreach;
14. public authority evasion claims.

## Section 2. Powers

The Constitutional Court may:

1. invalidate unconstitutional laws;
2. issue constitutional injunctions;
3. require lawful process;
4. enforce Kernel protections;
5. resolve federalism disputes;
6. review emergency powers;
7. require compliance plans;
8. review stewardship overreach;
9. protect opposition and party role rights;
10. enforce court order compliance.

## Section 3. Limits

The Constitutional Court may not:

1. govern ordinary policy;
2. choose governing coalitions;
3. set ordinary budget priorities;
4. select party platforms;
5. administer agencies directly except as necessary for constitutional remedy;
6. substitute judicial preference for lawful democratic choice.

## Section 4. Remedial Restraint

Where multiple lawful democratic options remain, the Court shall remedy the constitutional defect and leave policy choice to elected institutions.

---

# III. High Court / Supreme Appellate Court

## Section 1. Function

The High Court or Supreme Appellate Court is the final ordinary appellate court for non-constitutional civil, criminal, statutory, and procedural matters.

## Section 2. Jurisdiction

It may hear:

1. civil appeals;
2. criminal appeals;
3. statutory interpretation questions;
4. conflicts between lower courts;
5. ordinary administrative appeals where constitutional issues are not primary;
6. procedural questions of general importance.

## Section 3. Constitutional Questions

Where an ordinary appeal raises a substantial constitutional, Kernel, framework, emergency, federalism, or rights-floor issue, the matter may be referred to or reviewed by the Constitutional Court according to law.

---

# IV. Administrative Courts

## Section 1. Function

Administrative Courts review public agency action, administrative decision-making, and public-service disputes.

## Section 2. Jurisdiction

Administrative Courts may hear matters involving:

1. public benefits;
2. licensing;
3. taxation administration;
4. public housing;
5. education placement;
6. healthcare access;
7. agency enforcement;
8. civil penalties;
9. procurement decisions;
10. civil service disputes;
11. public records administration;
12. public-service denial or delay;
13. regulatory compliance.

## Section 3. Review Standards

Administrative action must be:

1. legally authorized;
2. reasoned;
3. non-arbitrary;
4. procedurally fair;
5. rights-compliant;
6. publicly accountable;
7. auditable where technology is used.

## Section 4. Technology or Rights Referral

Where an administrative matter materially involves rights-impacting automation, unlawful surveillance, public AI, or algorithmic harm, the matter may be referred to the Technology Review Tribunal or Constitutional Court as appropriate.

---

# V. Fast Rights Tribunal

## Section 1. Function

The Fast Rights Tribunal hears urgent rights claims where delay may cause serious or irreversible harm.

## Section 2. Jurisdiction

The Tribunal may hear urgent claims involving:

1. voting access;
2. ballot access;
3. unlawful detention;
4. due process denial;
5. censorship or speech restriction;
6. unlawful surveillance;
7. protest restriction;
8. emergency overreach;
9. discrimination;
10. benefit termination causing severe harm;
11. algorithmic denial without meaningful review;
12. denial of court access;
13. local or state rights floor violations.

## Section 3. Serious Claim Gate

Fast rights relief requires:

1. plausible serious rights claim;
2. imminent or difficult-to-repair harm;
3. connection to public authority;
4. inadequate ordinary remedy;
5. request for proportional relief.

## Section 4. Narrow Interim Relief

The Tribunal shall use the narrowest effective interim remedy.

Remedies may include:

1. individual pause;
2. limited class pause;
3. procedural correction;
4. temporary restoration of benefit or status;
5. temporary suspension of unlawful enforcement;
6. technology suspension where unauditable or unsafe;
7. emergency order narrowing;
8. broader program suspension only where severe systemic rights harm exists.

## Section 5. Anti-Abuse

Fast rights process may not be used in bad faith to paralyze ordinary administration, delay elections, harass public officials, or block lawful policy disagreement without serious rights harm.

---

# VI. Election and Party Court

## Section 1. Function

The Election and Party Court protects democratic competition and resolves election, districting, party-role, and campaign-process disputes.

## Section 2. Jurisdiction

The Court may hear:

1. election certification disputes;
2. ballot access disputes;
3. districting challenges;
4. campaign finance enforcement appeals;
5. party finance enforcement appeals;
6. party role assignment disputes;
7. common-control findings;
8. coalition status disputes;
9. debate access disputes;
10. public funding eligibility disputes;
11. party ledger corrections;
12. delayed self-dealing violations;
13. emergency election procedure disputes.

## Section 3. Election Certification Continuity

Election litigation may correct unlawful results or procedures, but may not create indefinite certification paralysis.

## Section 4. Required Speed

Election disputes shall be resolved quickly enough to preserve election integrity, voter rights, peaceful transfer, and public trust.

## Section 5. Bad-Faith Election Litigation

Courts may sanction knowingly false, repetitive, or bad-faith election claims designed to delay certification or undermine lawful results.

---

# VII. Technology Review Tribunal

## Section 1. Function

The Technology Review Tribunal reviews public algorithms, automated decision systems, AI, surveillance tools, public data systems, and technology-enabled public authority.

## Section 2. Jurisdiction

The Tribunal may hear disputes involving:

1. algorithmic benefit denial;
2. automated public-service prioritization;
3. predictive policing tools;
4. surveillance systems;
5. public AI procurement;
6. model audit disputes;
7. human review failures;
8. data misuse;
9. algorithmic discrimination;
10. black-box public authority;
11. model drift;
12. unauthorized self-learning;
13. public technology rollback;
14. technology registry noncompliance.

## Section 3. Powers

The Tribunal may:

1. require disclosure;
2. order meaningful human review;
3. suspend unsafe or unlawful systems;
4. require model audit;
5. require rollback to prior model version;
6. require correction of biased or unlawful systems;
7. require system-card correction;
8. refer constitutional questions to the Constitutional Court.

## Section 4. Limits

The Tribunal may not choose ordinary policy values, approve secret public authority, or substitute technical judgment for democratic judgment where lawful policy choices remain.

---

# VIII. One-Door Filing and Wrong-Door Transfer

## Section 1. One-Door Filing

A timely and plausible claim filed in the wrong court or tribunal in good faith shall be transferred to the proper forum, not dismissed.

## Section 2. Wrong-Door Transfer

A good-faith filing, appeal, complaint, records request, or rights petition made through the wrong public office shall be routed to the proper forum.

## Section 3. Transfer Deadline

Constitutional statute shall define deadlines for transfer.

Urgent rights, election, emergency, detention, or time-sensitive claims shall be transferred immediately or within an expedited period.

## Section 4. Anti-Circular-Transfer Rule

A matter may not be transferred indefinitely between offices or systems.

Where routing is disputed, a provisional responsible forum or accountable office shall be identified.

## Section 5. Preservation of Timeliness

A claim filed in good faith in the wrong forum shall preserve filing date for limitations, appeal, or emergency-review purposes where the claim is timely and plausible.

---

# IX. Primary Harm Rule

## Section 1. Purpose

The Primary Harm Rule assigns lead jurisdiction according to the main harm or authority at issue.

## Section 2. Default Lead Forums

| Primary harm or authority | Lead forum |
|---|---|
| Immediate rights harm | Fast Rights Tribunal |
| Election process harm | Election and Party Court |
| Algorithmic or public technology harm | Technology Review Tribunal |
| Agency action or public-service harm | Administrative Court |
| Constitutional Kernel or framework harm | Constitutional Court |
| Ordinary civil or criminal appeal | High Court / ordinary appellate path |

## Section 3. Multiple Harms

Where multiple harms are materially implicated, courts may coordinate, refer, or convene joint review according to law.

One court or tribunal shall remain publicly responsible for immediate routing and interim relief.

## Section 4. Constitutional Priority

Where a case implicates the Democratic Kernel, rights floor, emergency power, peaceful transfer, or court independence, constitutional review shall remain available.

---

# X. Urgent Review Timelines

## Section 1. Purpose

Urgent rights, election, emergency, and detention matters require review before harm becomes irreversible.

## Section 2. Required Timeline Categories

Constitutional statute shall establish expedited timelines for:

1. emergency petitions;
2. election certification disputes;
3. ballot access disputes;
4. districting disputes;
5. detention claims;
6. emergency surveillance challenges;
7. emergency-power renewal challenges;
8. Fast Rights Tribunal petitions;
9. technology suspensions where rights are at immediate risk.

## Section 3. Timeline Standards

Timelines shall be:

1. fast enough to preserve practical remedy;
2. disciplined enough to prevent sabotage;
3. public where possible;
4. compatible with due process;
5. adaptable to the seriousness and urgency of the harm.

## Section 4. Emergency Review Clock

Emergency power and emergency rights cases shall receive review under a defined emergency review clock.

Renewal review shall occur before emergency extension where practicable.

---

# XI. Appeals

## Section 1. Appeal Rights

Court and tribunal decisions shall have appeal or review paths defined by constitutional statute.

## Section 2. Constitutional Appeal

A matter may be appealed or referred for constitutional review where it materially involves:

1. Democratic Kernel;
2. rights floor;
3. emergency powers;
4. federalism;
5. election structure;
6. amendment classification;
7. court independence;
8. algorithmic sovereignty;
9. security neutrality;
10. public authority evasion.

## Section 3. Expedited Appeals

Expedited appeals shall exist for:

1. election matters;
2. emergency matters;
3. detention;
4. urgent rights;
5. imminent technology harm;
6. transfer of power disputes.

## Section 4. Finality

Finality rules shall preserve legal certainty without allowing unresolved serious rights or constitutional harms to evade review.

---

# XII. Remedial Hierarchy

## Section 1. Principle

Courts shall generally prefer the least intrusive remedy sufficient to cure the violation.

## Section 2. Remedy Types

Remedies may include:

1. notice;
2. disclosure;
3. procedural correction;
4. reconsideration;
5. individual relief;
6. limited class relief;
7. narrow injunction;
8. broader injunction where systemic harm exists;
9. compliance plan;
10. structural remedy only where necessary to correct persistent constitutional failure.

## Section 3. Structural Remedies

Structural remedies require:

1. serious constitutional violation;
2. persistent or systemic failure;
3. inadequacy of lesser remedies;
4. clear compliance standard;
5. defined review period;
6. respect for lawful democratic choice where possible.

## Section 4. Policy Choice

Courts shall leave lawful policy choices to democratic institutions where multiple constitutional options remain.

---

# XIII. Independent Technical Expert Pool

## Section 1. Purpose

Courts and tribunals may require technical expertise, especially in technology, fiscal, infrastructure, election, security, and algorithmic cases.

## Section 2. Pool Governance

An independent technical expert pool shall be governed by:

1. conflict disclosure;
2. rotating assignments;
3. vendor cooling-off rules;
4. public-interest participation;
5. academic and civil-society expertise;
6. professional competence;
7. adversarial challenge;
8. public explanation of technical assumptions where possible.

## Section 3. Anti-Capture

Technical experts shall assist courts.

They shall not colonize judicial judgment or substitute technical preference for constitutional reasoning.

## Section 4. Expert Challenge

Parties shall have a fair opportunity to challenge technical assumptions, conflicts, and expert conclusions.

---

# XIV. Court Order Enforcement

## Section 1. Enforcement Duty

Public authorities shall comply with final court and tribunal orders.

## Section 2. Enforcement Ladder

Failure to comply may trigger:

1. compliance deadline;
2. public noncompliance notice;
3. responsible-officer identification;
4. remedial order;
5. budget or administrative compliance review;
6. contempt or sanction where lawful;
7. Integrity System referral if bad faith exists;
8. legislative oversight trigger for persistent noncompliance.

## Section 3. Court Compliance Dashboard

The Public Constitutional Interface shall include a court compliance dashboard showing:

1. final rights and constitutional orders;
2. responsible offices;
3. compliance deadlines;
4. current status;
5. lawful exceptions;
6. escalation steps.

## Section 4. Bad-Faith Noncompliance

Bad-faith refusal to comply with final rights or constitutional orders may trigger integrity review, legislative oversight, or removal process where legally appropriate.

---

# XV. Judicial Structure Change Rule

## Section 1. Covered Changes

Changes to court size, jurisdiction, appointment process, tenure, tribunal powers, or case-allocation rules are judicial structure changes.

## Section 2. Required Process

Judicial structure changes require:

1. enhanced process;
2. public impact statement;
3. delayed effect where power rules are implicated;
4. constitutional review where Kernel rights are affected;
5. public explanation;
6. protection against immediate partisan benefit.

## Section 3. Reform and Capture

Court reform is permitted.

Court capture is not.

## Section 4. Delayed Self-Dealing

A governing bloc may not change judicial structure for its own immediate advantage in pending or foreseeable disputes involving its power.

---

# XVI. Access to Justice

## Section 1. Public Guidance

The Rights and Justice System shall publish plain-language guidance explaining:

1. which forum handles which claim;
2. filing steps;
3. deadlines;
4. appeal paths;
5. urgent review paths;
6. technology-related rights;
7. election deadlines;
8. available remedies.

## Section 2. Legal Assistance

Constitutional statute shall provide access support for high-risk rights claims, including where needed:

1. legal aid;
2. filing assistance;
3. translation;
4. disability access;
5. non-digital access;
6. public navigators.

## Section 3. No Practical Denial

A right shall not be practically denied because the person cannot navigate court complexity.

---

# XVII. Abuse Prevention

## Section 1. Frivolous or Bad-Faith Claims

Courts may sanction:

1. knowingly false claims;
2. repetitive harassment filings;
3. bad-faith emergency petitions;
4. fabricated evidence;
5. strategic obstruction with no plausible legal basis.

## Section 2. Government Delay

Courts may sanction or correct:

1. refusal to produce records;
2. missing appeal notices;
3. failure to implement orders;
4. use of complexity to evade review;
5. algorithmic opacity;
6. improper emergency secrecy;
7. bad-faith transfer between forums.

## Section 3. Balance

Courts shall remain open to serious claims and closed to procedural sabotage.

---

# XVIII. Schedule Review

## Section 1. Periodic Review

This Schedule shall be reviewed periodically to ensure the court system remains accessible, independent, timely, and capable of protecting rights.

## Section 2. Review Criteria

Review shall assess:

1. time to remedy;
2. access to courts;
3. filing success;
4. wrong-door transfer success;
5. urgent review speed;
6. appeal clarity;
7. court order compliance;
8. technical expert independence;
9. public usability;
10. risk of judicial overreach;
11. risk of judicial capture.

## Section 3. Amendment

Amendment of this Schedule shall follow the major statutory architecture or framework amendment process, as applicable.
